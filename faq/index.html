TRACKERS, NOTES, BOXES & RESOURCES

What is a study tracker?

It's a day-by-day plan for one HTB certification. Every module in the cert's job-role path is split into daily tasks, in the order you should study them, with practice, review days and exam prep mixed in. You check tasks off as you go, and the dashboard shows how far along you are and whether you're on pace.

Each tracker also has a Resources tab (videos, exam reviews, notes, tools and tips from people who passed) and an About tab explaining how the cert and exam work.

Every cert has its own page on this site with four things on it: the tracker, matching prep notes for Obsidian, Notion or CherryTree, a list of practice boxes (see the next two questions), and a Resources page with every study link in one place, plus my top picks and what the cert costs.

What are the prep notes?

They're the notes half of the kit: a ready-made notes setup for one cert, in the app you already use. Each cert's page links to its Notes page, where you pick Obsidian, Notion or CherryTree. All three have the same notes inside:

  • A Methodology section with ten blank, numbered phases. You rename them to the steps of your own workflow and fill them in as you learn.
  • A note for every Academy module in the cert's path, in course order. Each is split into one part per section, ready for your commands and screenshots.
  • An Exam section for exam days: targets, credentials, findings, flags and a log for each day, laid out to match the cert's report so the report half-writes itself.
  • Links to community cheat sheets for that cert, plus reporting prep: the cert's SysReptor template and the reporting module.

Getting them depends on the app. For Obsidian, download the zip, unzip it and choose Open folder as vault. It only uses Obsidian's built-in features, so you don't need any plugins. For Notion, open the template and click Duplicate to copy it into your workspace (you'll need a free Notion account). For CherryTree, download the .ctd file and open it with File → Open. CherryTree comes with Kali, so you may already have it.

All three work offline once you have your copy. In Notion that means the desktop or mobile app, after you mark the pages Available offline. If you'd like to look inside first, every Obsidian note is on GitHub.

Once you start writing, your copy is yours. Later updates on this site won't touch it. Back up the Obsidian folder or the .ctd file like any other file (a cloud drive or Git works). Notion keeps your copy in your workspace.

What are practice boxes?

It's a list of HTB Labs boxes picked for one cert, grouped from easiest to hardest. Each box shows which Academy modules it practices, links to walkthroughs (IppSec videos and 0xdf write-ups, where they exist), and who recommended it: HTB's prep track for the cert, community prep playlists, or reviews from people who passed. Tick each one off as you solve it.

You'll find them on each cert's page and in the tracker's Practice Boxes tab. On the site, both show the same list and the same progress, and the tracker's Export backup includes it.

They're optional. Everything you need to pass is in the cert's Academy path. Boxes let you practice your methodology without step-by-step help, and each one is a chance to write a mock report. Most need VIP+ (see the question on Free, VIP+ and Pro Labs below).

The CDSA and CDEP lists are Sherlocks instead of machines. HTB hasn't recommended any for the CDEP yet, so that list is my best guess, and the page says so.

Is this made by Hack The Box? Does it cost anything?

No to both. These are free, independent study aids, not affiliated with or endorsed by Hack The Box. You don't need an account or a sign-up. The HTB content itself (Academy modules, exam vouchers, lab subscriptions) is paid and comes from HTB.

Which timeline should I pick?

Each tracker offers five timelines, built for roughly 50, 40, 30, 20 or 10 hours of study a week. Pick the one that matches the hours you can really give it, not the hours you wish you had. Bigger certs take more days at the same pace. The CJCA, for example, runs from about 25 days at 50 hours a week to about 130 days at 10.

You can switch later without losing anything. Your checked tasks and notes stay attached to the tasks themselves, so they move with you to the new timeline.

The cert picker also suggests one. Answer its hours question, and the Start the plan button on your result (for example "Start the 160-day CPTS plan") opens the tracker on the timeline that fits.

Where do the time estimates come from, and how accurate are they?

They come from Hack The Box. Every module in HTB Academy shows an estimated completion time on its cert's path page, and the trackers use those official numbers. Each module's hours are spread across its days by section, and the timelines divide the path's total hours by your weekly hours.

Treat them as a minimum. Many learners find a module takes longer than HTB's estimate, especially when you're new to the topic, take thorough notes, or get stuck on a skills assessment. That's normal and doesn't mean you're slow.

If you keep running over, the pace box will show you're behind. Switch to a longer timeline. Your progress carries over, and it's better to plan for your real pace than to fight the calendar.

I fell behind. What now?

Everyone does. The pace box on the dashboard tells you whether you're ahead, on pace or behind, based on your start date. If you're behind you can:

  • Use the catch-up days built into the plan.
  • Switch to a longer timeline. Your progress carries over.
  • Change your start date to give yourself a fresh baseline.

The plan is there to keep you moving. Nobody's grading you on it.

Where is my progress saved?

In your browser, on your device. Nothing is sent anywhere. That has a few consequences worth knowing:

  • Progress on your computer won't show up on your phone or on another browser.
  • Clearing your browser's site data (or cookies and site data) erases it.
  • Private or incognito windows forget everything when you close them.

That's why the Export backup button exists. See the next question. Practice boxes are saved the same way, and the tracker's backup includes them.

How do backups work? How do I move to another computer?

Click Export backup in the tracker. It downloads a small file named like cjca-tracker-backup.json with your start date, timeline, checked tasks and notes. On the other computer, open the same tracker, click Import and choose that file.

Export one every week or so and keep it somewhere safe, like a cloud drive or your notes folder. If you ever clear your browser, importing it puts you right back where you were.

Should I use the website or download the file?

Either works. Opening a tracker on this site is the quickest way to start. The Download .html button on each cert's page saves the tracker as a single HTML file that runs offline in any browser.

One catch: the website and a downloaded file keep separate progress, because your browser treats them as different places. Pick one and stick with it, or move between them with Export and Import.

The website version also gets updates, like new resources, as soon as they're published. On rare occasions an update changes a tracker's plan in a way that affects its timeline, and your days might shift along with it. A downloaded file never changes, so it keeps your plan exactly as it was, but it also won't get new resources unless you download it again.

Practice boxes work the same way: use the page on the site, or save it with Download this page. On the site, your ticks also show in the tracker's Practice Boxes tab.

I already finished some modules. Do I start from day one?

No. Open Already done some modules? in the tracker and tick the modules you've completed. The tracker checks off those tasks for you. If you've passed another HTB cert, the Passed another cert? shortcuts tick every module the two paths share.

Clicking a day's DAY badge also marks that whole day done in one click, which is handy for catching up on days you did but didn't tick.

What do "Free", "VIP+" and "Pro Labs" mean next to a resource or box?

They tell you what you need to access a practice lab on the main HTB platform (not the Academy). Free means any HTB account can play it. VIP+ means it's retired, and retired machines and challenges need a paid VIP+ subscription. HTB does keep a small, rotating set of retired machines free, so a VIP+ box might be free for a while when you look. Pro Labs (marked HTB PRO in the practice boxes) need an HTB PRO subscription, which includes 10 of them (Dante, Zephyr and Offshore among them).

Labels can also go out of date. Active machines are free, but when one retires it becomes VIP+, so a box marked Free here might need VIP+ by the time you get to it. HTB's HTB Labs Subscriptions page has the current details for each plan.

None of these are required to pass. They're extra practice.

A link is broken, or I know a great resource. Who do I tell?

Use Suggest a resource or Report a problem on GitHub. Both work for the trackers, the prep notes and the practice boxes. You'll need a free GitHub account.

HTB BASICS

What's the difference between HTB Academy and HTB Labs?

HTB Academy (academy.hackthebox.com) is where the courses live. Its modules teach a topic step by step, with small labs and questions to answer. Certifications are earned through the Academy.

HTB Labs, the main hackthebox.com platform, is where the "boxes" are: full practice machines you attack without instructions. They're great for practice, but separate from the Academy and billed separately. Each cert's practice boxes pick out the ones worth doing for that exam.

What are job-role paths, modules and sections?

A module is one course on one topic, like "Linux Fundamentals" or "Network Enumeration with Nmap". Each module is split into sections, short lessons that often end with a question you answer from a lab.

A job-role path is HTB's ordered list of modules for a role. Each certification has one, and you have to finish the whole path before the exam unlocks. The About tab in each tracker says how big that cert's path is.

What's a skills assessment?

The last section of many modules. Instead of step-by-step questions, you get a scenario and have to use what the module taught to solve it on your own. It's the closest thing to exam practice inside the path. If one feels hard, redo it before the exam. The trackers schedule time for that.

What are cubes, subscriptions and vouchers?

Cubes are the Academy's currency for unlocking modules one at a time. A subscription unlocks modules up to a tier every month instead. The exam is a separate purchase called a voucher, and some annual plans include one. Prices and plans change, so check HTB's own pricing page before you buy.

How many attempts a voucher gives you, and how retakes work, varies by cert. Each tracker's About tab covers it.

Which cert should I start with?

If you're new to security, start with the CJCA. It's HTB's entry-level cert and covers both attacking and defending, so you'll find out which side you like. After it, most people move on to the CPTS for pentesting or the CDSA for defense.

Already have some experience or another cert? The cert picker asks five quick questions and gives you the cert that fits, what to do after it, a study plan for your weekly hours and roughly what it costs.

What's the cheapest way to get a cert?

Pay monthly, finish the path as fast as you can, and buy the voucher on its own when you're ready for the exam. Every month you cut saves a month of the plan.

  • Got a student email? The Student plan is $10 a month and reaches Tier II, so it covers the CJCA, CPTS, CWES, CDSA and COAE.
  • Monthly beats annual unless you'd be paying monthly for about a year. An annual plan only makes sense if you need that long, or if you'll use the extra voucher it comes with.
  • Pick the cheapest plan that reaches your cert's tier. Gold reaches Tier III, but you don't need it for a Tier II cert.

What are machines, boxes and flags?

A machine (or box) is a computer set up for you to break into legally. A flag is a string of text hidden on it that proves you got in. Usually there's a user flag for getting a foothold and a root flag for taking full control (privilege escalation). Many HTB exams are built from machines like these, so practicing on boxes is good exam prep. A Sherlock is the defensive version: you get logs or a disk image and work out what happened.

A writeup is someone's walkthrough of how they solved a box. Try the box first, then read a writeup to see what you missed.

YOUR SETUP

What is an "attack box"? Pwnbox or my own VM?

An attack box is the Linux machine you work from, loaded with security tools. You have two options:

  • Pwnbox is HTB's attack box that runs in your browser: a Parrot Linux desktop with the tools already installed and already on HTB's network. Any paid Academy plan or purchase includes unlimited Pwnbox time, but each session shuts down eventually, so keep your notes outside it. HTB's Introduction to Pwnbox shows how to start one.
  • Your own virtual machine running Kali Linux or Parrot OS, inside free software like VirtualBox or VMware Workstation. It takes an hour to set up, but it's yours, it keeps your files, and it has no time limit. HTB's guide to installing Parrot on a VM lists the settings to use, and Kali has its own VirtualBox guide.

Pwnbox is the easiest way to start. Most people move to their own VM before the exam, since exams run for days.

How do I connect to the labs with the VPN?

If you're not using Pwnbox, your VM has to join HTB's lab network. In the Academy, open a lab section, choose to connect with VPN, and download the .ovpn file. Then in your VM's terminal:

sudo openvpn ~/Downloads/academy-regular.ovpn

Use whatever name your file actually has. Leave that terminal open while you work. When it says "Initialization Sequence Completed", you're connected, and the lab's target IP should respond. Don't run Pwnbox and your own VPN connection at the same time.

HTB's Connecting to Academy VPN article walks through each step, and Connection Troubleshooting covers the common errors if it won't connect.

NOTES & REPORTS

Why do I need notes if I can just reread the modules?

Because the exam is a race against the clock, and you need to find a command in seconds, not dig through your whole path. Your notes are also where your report comes from. Take notes on every module from day one: the exact commands that worked and a screenshot of the result.

Which note-taking app should I choose?

The popular ones for this kind of study are Obsidian, Joplin, CherryTree and Notion. They all work, and the best one is the one you'll actually keep using. The prep notes on this site come for three of them (Obsidian, Notion and CherryTree), so you can start with whichever you pick. TCM Security's The BEST Notetaking Apps Ranked (9 minutes) compares them with pros and cons for each.

Personally, I use Obsidian. It's free, the notes are plain text files you own, and it handles code blocks and screenshots well. That's why the next answer walks through setting it up.

How do I set up Obsidian?

Obsidian is a free note-taking app. Your notes are plain Markdown text files in a normal folder on your computer, so they're never locked inside an app.

The quickest start is your cert's prep notes. Install Obsidian, download the zip from the cert's Notes page, unzip it and choose Open folder as vault. It's already set up with the folders, module notes and screenshot settings below. If you'd rather build your own from scratch, here's how:

  1. Download it from obsidian.md and install it.
  2. Choose Create new vault. A vault is just the folder your notes live in. Name it after the cert, like CJCA.
  3. Make a few folders: Modules (one note per module), Methodology (see below), Screenshots, and Exam.
  4. In Settings → Files and links, set the default location for new attachments to your Screenshots folder. Now screenshots you paste land there instead of cluttering everything.
  5. Start your first module note. Headings start with #, and commands go in code blocks between two lines of three backticks (```), so they stay copyable.

A simple note for each module is plenty: what the module is about, the commands that worked (with a line on what each does), gotchas, and screenshots. You don't need plugins or themes to start. Plenty of people over-build their Obsidian setup and study less.

Back up the vault folder like any other folder (a cloud drive or Git works).

What is a methodology?

You keep two things while you study: notes and a methodology. Notes follow the course, one per module. Your methodology is your personal playbook, built from those notes but organized by what you're trying to do, not by which module taught it. It answers the question you'll actually have mid-exam, like "I found an SMB share, now what?"

Each page covers one technique or service and has:

  • A short explanation of when to use it.
  • A table of the commands, with what each one does.
  • What to look for in the output, plus the next step.

For example, a page called SMB might list how to find shares, connect to them, and what to try if anonymous login works. Build it as you study: when you finish a module, copy the commands that worked from your notes into the right page. Most HTB Academy modules also have a Cheat Sheet button above the table of contents, a PDF of the module's commands, which helps when you write your notes. By exam day, any technique should be one lookup away. It works for any cert, and it's worth starting with your very first module. The prep notes leave their Methodology section blank for this reason, so you can build it your own way.

If you use Obsidian, blue-pho3nix/pentesting_templates_obsidian is a good template to start from. It's a set of Obsidian templates organized the same way, by phase and then one page per service or technique (it even has an SMB page). Its README walks you through setup: copy the templates into your vault, turn on the free Templater community plugin, and insert a template with Alt+E. It was built for CPTS and OSCP prep, so treat it as a structure to copy and fill with the commands you learn in your own path.

What is SysReptor, and why does the report matter so much?

HTB exams aren't just about the hands-on part. Every exam ends with a professional report that HTB staff grade, and a weak report can fail you even if you got enough points.

SysReptor is a reporting tool that has official templates for HTB exams. You write your findings in it and it produces a polished PDF. You can run it yourself, or use SysReptor's free cloud for HTB students, explained on their Hack The Box Reporting page. If you use the cloud, export your report when you're done, because inactive accounts are deleted after three months. SysReptor also publishes a sample report for each HTB cert (here's the CJCA one), and every tracker links its cert's sample under Reporting & Exam Prep. Read yours before you start the exam, since it shows the structure and depth HTB expects.

For a walkthrough, Cyber Ryan's CPTS Report Made EASY video covers setting up SysReptor and using the HTB template, and dollarboysushil's report writing guide shows what each section of a finished report looks like. Both use the CPTS, but the process is the same for every HTB cert.

Whatever cert you're taking, do the Documentation & Reporting module from the Penetration Tester path. It's already part of the CPTS path; for other certs you add it yourself. It's built around the CPTS report, so it won't match your cert's report one for one, but the note-taking habits and report tips carry over to every exam.

Practice once before exam day: pick an easy box (your cert's practice boxes start with easy ones), solve it, and write a short report about it in SysReptor. Several exam reviews in the Resources tab say the same thing: start writing during the exam, not on the last night.
